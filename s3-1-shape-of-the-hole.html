Chapter 3.1

The Shape
of the Hole

Thirty-eight percent of revenue rests on one face.

The portfolio changed shape while it grew.

In 2024 Pop Mart looked like a portfolio company. Its largest IP contributed 23.3% of revenue and its second contributed 16.1% — a reasonably distributed book of characters, none individually decisive. Twelve months later the largest contributes 38.1% and the second contributes 9.5%.

Nothing shrank. Every major IP grew, several by more than 150%. The concentration is not the result of failure elsewhere — it is the result of one character growing 365.7% while everything else grew merely very fast.

Concentration is usually presented as self-evidently bad. Here it is not, and an analysis that only makes the bear case is not doing the work. Management's position — that THE MONSTERS is a family of characters rather than a single hit, and that the incubation engine is demonstrably still producing — is supported by real numbers in the same disclosure.

The concentration case
One face is carrying the year
  • THE MONSTERS at RMB 14.16bn earns more than SKULLPANDA, CRYBABY, MOLLY and DIMOO combined.
  • Concentration rose in the year the portfolio was supposed to broaden — 23.3% to 38.1%.
  • MOLLY, the classic IP and the intended second curve, grew 38.4% against a group average of 184.7%. It is now the fourth largest.
  • A character-led business has no mechanism to defend against taste. Nothing in the supply chain, the store network or the app protects a face that stops being wanted.
The platform case
The engine produced a new one
  • 星星人 grew from RMB 120m to RMB 2.06bn in a single year — up 1,602%, and now larger than HIRONO. It was signed in August 2024.
  • Six IPs cleared RMB 2bn and seventeen cleared RMB 100m. That is a roster, not a single act.
  • Artist-originated IP rose to 90.0% of revenue from 85.3% — the growth came from characters the company controls, not licences it rents.
  • THE MONSTERS is a family — LABUBU, ZIMOMO, MOKOKO — not one product line. Its internal composition is more diversified than the segment label suggests.

The honest position sits between them, and it is uncomfortable for both sides. 星星人 is a single observation. One IP going from nothing to RMB 2bn in twelve months either demonstrates a repeatable incubation capability or records a fortunate year in an unusually favourable market. The disclosures cannot distinguish between those readings, and neither can anyone outside the company.

How much has to be replaced, and by how many.

The way to size a concentration risk is not to argue about whether the hit will fade. It is to ask what standing still would require if it did. Set a decline for THE MONSTERS and see how many new IPs of a given size are needed to hold group revenue flat.

Deliberately static: it holds every other IP flat and ignores the operating leverage and channel growth described in Sections One and Two. Its only purpose is to convert a percentage into a number of characters — because characters, not percentages, are what the company has to produce.

So what
The risk is not that the hit fades. It is that nobody knows the replacement rate.

Every character-driven business lives with decay; that is the category, not a defect. Sanrio has watched Hello Kitty rise and fall across five decades. What separates a franchise from a fad is not whether the current hit lasts, but whether the organisation reliably produces the next one before it is needed.

Pop Mart has exactly one clean recent data point on that question, and it is a good one: 星星人, signed in August 2024, at RMB 2.06bn twelve months later. Against it sits MOLLY at +38.4% — the classic IP, given every advantage of scale and distribution, growing at a fifth of the group rate. One engine started; one did not restart.

A company that cannot yet demonstrate its replacement rate has two ways to raise it: build faster, or buy. Pop Mart has never bought. Three companies that faced this same question — and answered it three different ways — are the subject of 3.2.
