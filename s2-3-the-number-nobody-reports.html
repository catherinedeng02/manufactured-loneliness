Chapter 2.3

The Number
Nobody Reports

China's repeat rate is disclosed. Nowhere else's is.

In China, the habit is measurable — and it is real.

The mainland membership programme is the most convincing asset in the business, and the company reports it in detail. At the end of 2025 there were 72.58 million registered members, up 26.5 million in a year. Members accounted for 93.7% of sales. And the repeat purchase rate reached 55.7% — up from 50% two years earlier, while the customer base more than doubled.

That last combination is the difficult one to achieve. Repeat rates usually fall when a base expands quickly, because new cohorts dilute the loyal core. China's went the other way.

This is what a durable consumer franchise looks like on a page. Rising repeat purchase against a rapidly growing base means the cohorts joining in 2024 and 2025 are behaving at least as well as the ones already there. The China business has proved it.

There is nothing improper about this. The mainland programme has been running for over a decade; the overseas business is roughly two years old and was reorganised onto a four-region structure only in 2025. Cohort data takes time to become meaningful, and publishing a repeat rate built on six months of customers would be worse than publishing none.

But the absence is still information. The entire overseas thesis rests on the assumption that these markets will eventually behave like China — and the single metric that would demonstrate it is the one that does not exist yet.

The 2.1 finding sharpens this. In the Americas, 64% of revenue arrived online, much of it through feeds and a first-party app that launched recently. Feed-acquired customers are, by construction, disproportionately first-time buyers in the year the feed discovers you.

How much of 2026 has to be bought again.

Since the number is not disclosed, the useful exercise is to size what it would need to be. Management has guided to group revenue growth of not less than 20% in 2026. Assume China and overseas both grow at that rate. Then move the dial below through the range of plausible overseas repeat rates and watch what happens to the amount of revenue that must come from customers who have never bought before.

A deliberately crude bridge: it treats the repeat rate as the share of prior-year revenue that recurs, and holds spend per returning customer flat. Real cohort behaviour is more complex. The point is the order of magnitude, not the decimal.

So what
Arrive and Belong are proven. Repeat is still an assumption.

This section is named for three things a consumer has to do before a brand becomes a franchise. The 2025 disclosures demonstrate the first two conclusively: they arrived in enormous numbers, and in China at least, they clearly belong — 93.7% of sales to members, with repeat purchase rising through a doubling of the base.

The third is unproven outside China, and it is the one that determines whether 2025 was a step-change or a very large first purchase. An analyst cannot resolve it from public information. What an analyst can do is state plainly which parts of the valuation depend on it, and refuse to let a group-level growth rate stand in for a cohort metric it does not contain.

The practical response is not to assume the worst. It is to stop treating the four regions as one business with one set of economics — because on everything that is disclosed, they already are not. That is 2.4.
