Chapter 1.1

Why Did It
Suddenly Get So Loud?

Everyone credits social media, but the data disagrees.

Four things had to move at once.

A cultural phenomenon is not one event. It is four systems firing in sequence, and the order matters more than the magnitude. If we decompose the 2024–25 revenue explosion into its component drivers, the question stops being why did it happen and becomes which one moved first.

Moved first

Drivers 01–03 are widely discussed and easy to observe. Driver 04 is the one that can be tested against the financial statements — which is what the rest of this chapter does.

The margin test.

When a company is overrun by demand it did not plan for, the cost line tells on it. Expedited production, air freight, overtime, third-party sourcing at spot prices — a genuine demand shock compresses gross margin even as revenue climbs. That is the signature to look for.

Pop Mart's revenue grew 184.7% in 2025. Gross margin did not compress. It expanded by 5.3 points, to its highest level on record.

Which one moved first.

Sequence is the cleanest evidence available. If social amplification were the cause, the operational build-out would follow it. In practice the build-out came earlier — the format, the supplier structure, and the channel footprint were all in place before the loudest months arrived.

Hover a row to isolate it. Blue markers denote supply-side moves; amber markers denote demand-side or attention-side events.

So what
Social media set the timing. The supply chain set the ceiling.

Attention is cheap and periodic; every consumer category gets its viral month. What separates a spike from a step-change is whether the operation can convert attention into shipped units without surrendering unit economics. Pop Mart's 2025 margin expansion is the evidence that it could — and it is the reason the revenue line moved to a new level rather than a new peak.

The strategic implication is uncomfortable for the bull case. If the binding constraint was never demand, then the next constraint is not attention either. It is whatever the supply system does to the thing that made it scarce — which is the subject of 1.3.
