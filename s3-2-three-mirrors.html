Chapter 3.2

Three Futures,
Already Lived

Funko, Sanrio and Disney each reached this point. None chose the same door.

Two companies, the same number, opposite directions.

Sanrio discloses that Hello Kitty now accounts for 37% of consolidated revenue. Pop Mart discloses that THE MONSTERS accounts for 38.1%. The figures are almost identical, and they mean opposite things.

Sanrio's own filings state that operating profit declined for seven consecutive fiscal years, from the year ended March 2015 through the year ended March 2021, and attribute it to business development reliant on a single core character. Pop Mart is at the same concentration ratio today — but at the beginning of the curve rather than the end of it.

Three answers to the same question.

Character concentration is not a novel problem. Three companies have faced a recognisable version of it, made three different decisions, and lived long enough for the outcomes to be visible. Select one.

The three doors, side by side.

Company The choice What it cost, and what it took
Sanrio Build Seven consecutive years of declining operating profit before the strategy changed. Roughly four more to recover. No acquisition — the second characters were grown internally and monetised through licensing.
Disney Buy Approximately USD 15.5bn across three acquisitions between 2006 and 2012. Bought creative organisations and their catalogues outright, then ran them at arm's length rather than integrating them.
Funko Neither Scaled production against licensed characters it did not own. Ended 2022 with USD 246m of inventory, up 48%, and destroyed USD 30–36m of it. FY2023 net loss of USD 154m.

One detail from the Funko case belongs in this project specifically. The surplus stock was physically destroyed rather than discounted, on the reasoning that selling a collectible at one or two dollars would destroy the secondary market and devalue the whole category. Funko paid roughly thirty million dollars in cash to protect scarcity — which is the argument of 1.3, priced and settled.

So what
Only one of the three bought its way out, and it was already the largest company in the room.

The comparison flatters the acquisition case less than expected. Sanrio solved character concentration without buying anything, and the cost was time — seven years of declining profit before the lesson landed. Disney solved it by acquisition, but it was purchasing creative organisations at a scale, and with a currency, available to almost nobody else. Funko demonstrates the third path, which is to keep scaling the hit and hope, and it is the only one of the three with a documented failure attached.

Pop Mart's position resembles Sanrio's structurally — it owns its characters, 90% of revenue comes from artist IP it controls, and its concentration ratio is currently the same. It resembles Disney's in only one respect, which is that it now has the balance sheet to consider buying. And it resembles Funko's in the one dimension that should worry a reader of Section One: it has just spent a year scaling production against a single character faster than any of these three ever did.

None of that answers what an acquisition would actually be for. If the company were to buy, the question is not how much — it is what specific gap the purchase closes, and whether any available target closes it. That is 3.3.
