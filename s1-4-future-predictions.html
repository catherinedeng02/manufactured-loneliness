What the 2020 model could not have seen coming.

Everything on the table in December 2020.

Hindsight makes every valuation look either timid or reckless. The only honest way to judge the IPO price is to rebuild the model with the information that existed on the day — and then look at what that information could not contain.

Build the 2020 model yourself.

Three assumptions carry almost all the weight: how fast revenue compounds, what margin the business settles at, and what multiple the market will pay for it at the end. Set them the way you would have in December 2020 — with a 101× trailing multiple on screen, a pandemic underway, and no evidence yet that any of this travels abroad.

The model was wrong twice, in opposite directions.

The listing-day forecast expected RMB 1.95bn of net profit by 2022. Actual 2022 net profit fell 44% year on year to roughly RMB 0.48bn — a four-fold overestimate. The share price followed: from a February 2021 peak near HK$107.6 to below HK$10 by the end of 2022, a market capitalisation collapse from roughly HK$150bn to HK$13bn.

The same model then proved catastrophically too conservative. Its 2022 profit forecast of RMB 1.95bn was surpassed nearly seven times over by 2025's adjusted RMB 13.08bn.

Between 2021 and 2022 revenue moved from RMB 4.49bn to RMB 4.62bn — growth of 2.8%. For two years the thesis looked broken, and by most reasonable measures it was. Everything that makes the story remarkable today happened after the point at which the market had already given up on it.

Four things no 2020 model contained.

Not because the analysts were careless, but because none of these were variables yet. They were not mis-forecast — they were absent from the model entirely.

Three of the four are demand-side surprises. The fourth is the supply-side capability that made the other three convertible into revenue — which is why it is the one worth understanding first.

So what
The IPO price was not wrong. The confidence interval was.

At HK$38.50 the market applied 101× trailing earnings to a company whose growth had just decelerated from 227% to 49% in the middle of a pandemic. Judged against the following two years, that was expensive. Judged against the following five, it was one of the cheapest entry points of the decade.

Both statements are true, which is the actual finding. The 2020 model produced a point estimate for a business whose value was governed by a small number of binary, unmodellable outcomes: whether a new format would land, whether the brand would travel, whether one character among dozens would become globally legible. Those are not variables you refine with a better multiple. They are variables you can only size, and then hold a position against.

The practical implication for anyone valuing this company today is uncomfortable, and it is the note S1 ends on: the same structure that made 2020's model useless makes 2026's model useless in the same way. The question is no longer whether the factory works. It is whether the next character arrives — and there is no line in any model for that.
