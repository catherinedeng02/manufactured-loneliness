Chapter 3.4

What Money
Cannot Buy

The missing asset may not be the kind of thing that is for sale.

Everything this company owns, and the one thing it doesn't.

Three sections of analysis produce a fairly complete inventory. Almost every input to this business is owned, controllable, or purchasable. One is not, and it is the one the entire structure rests on.

The asset register
Production capacity Outsourced, elastic, re-tenderable. Scaled several times over in a single year without capital expenditure. Rentable
Distribution 630 stores in twenty countries, 2,637 robot shops, a first-party app and marketplace presence. Buildable at will. Buildable
The characters Ninety percent artist-originated IP, contractually held. The company owns the faces outright. Owned
Creative pipeline Artist signings, incubation, internal design. Improvable, and acquirable through the archetypes in 3.3. Acquirable
Why anyone cares Held entirely by the people who bought them. Not contracted, not licensed, not on any balance sheet, and not transferable in any transaction. Not for sale
The company owns the character. The customer owns the attachment.
This is not a metaphor. It is the specific reason a fading IP cannot be repaired by spending, and the specific reason an acquired IP does not transfer its meaning to the acquirer — the meaning was never in the asset being sold. Disney bought Marvel's catalogue and organisation; it did not buy anyone's childhood, and it needed the childhoods to already exist.

Which produces the uncomfortable symmetry this project has been circling. A company built to industrialise companionship has run into the one component of companionship that resists industrialisation. It can manufacture the object. It cannot manufacture the caring.

But it leaves a trace, and the trace is already in the disclosures.

Attachment is not measurable directly. Its consequence is: characters that people continue to care about continue to earn, years after the season that made them fashionable has ended. That is observable in the FY2025 IP table, and almost nobody reads the table this way, because LABUBU's growth rate absorbs the attention.

Sort the same numbers by vintage rather than by size, and a different question gets answered.

FY2025 revenue by IP, ordered by approximate vintage
Nothing has gone to zero. MOLLY, introduced around 2016 and long past the season that made it famous, still earned RMB 2.90bn in 2025 — more than the entire company earned in 2020. DIMOO, from roughly 2019, earned RMB 2.78bn. SKULLPANDA, from around 2020, earned RMB 3.54bn and grew 170.6%. These are not the numbers of a portfolio that decays.

This does not dissolve the concentration problem in 3.1. THE MONSTERS is still 38.1% of revenue and the replacement rate is still unproven. But it substantially changes the shape of the risk. The bear case assumes decay. The disclosed record shows persistence. A 2016 character still earning nearly three billion renminbi is evidence that this company's faces do accumulate something — slowly, quietly, and entirely outside the growth-rate column everyone reads.

What to underwrite instead.

If the decisive asset cannot be bought, the strategic response is not acquisition and it is not resignation. It is to stop underwriting the wrong variable.

01
Underwrite persistence, not the next hit.
Every model examined in this project — the 2020 IPO model, the sell-side targets, the bear case on concentration — forecasts the arrival or absence of the next character. None of them forecasts how long the existing ones last, which is both more tractable and, on the evidence above, more favourable.
02
Disclose revenue by IP vintage.
The single most valuable disclosure this company could add costs nothing: IP revenue grouped by year of introduction. It would convert the central unknown of this analysis — does attachment accumulate here, or is each character a season? — from an argument into a fact. It is the answer to 2.3 and 3.1 at once.
03
Buy time, not characters.
On the 3.3 screen, the narrative archetype ranks first on almost any weighting. Its real function is not to create new faces but to slow the decay of the ones already owned — to do deliberately what nine years appear to have done for MOLLY by accident.
04
Protect the thing 1.3 identified.
The gravest risk in this business is not that a character fades. It is that the operation described in Section One removes the scarcity that made the character worth wanting, faster than any acquisition could replace it. The most valuable capital allocation decision available may be to produce less.
So what
Nothing is missing that can be bought. Something is missing that can only be waited for.

This section began by asking who a companion brand turns to when it grows lonely. The answer the analysis produces is that there is no counterparty, because the asset in question is not held by any seller. Meaning accrues to a character through the accumulated time of the people who carry it around, and the only way to acquire that is to have already spent the years.

Which is a strange conclusion to reach in a chapter about mergers, and the correct one. The company does not need to buy a character. It needs the characters it already owns to get older, and it needs to avoid the specific mistakes — overproducing into scarcity, mistaking arrival for belonging, reading a group growth rate as a strategy — that would prevent that from happening.

MOLLY is nine years old and still earning nearly three billion renminbi. That is the most under-discussed number in the entire disclosure, and it is the closest thing to proof that what this company sells is capable of lasting.

They built a factory that could make anything,
and a world that would buy it,
and then discovered the only thing that mattered
was whether anyone kept it.
As the world grew lonelier, people began to buy a friend.
Whether it stayed one was never the factory's decision.
