Chapter 1.3

The More You Make,
the Less It's Worth

The machine kept running after the magic ran out.

Scarcity is not a side effect. It is the product.

A blind box is not sold on its contents. It is sold on the gap between what you might get and what you probably will — a gap that exists only while supply is short. Queue photographs, empty shelves and resale premiums are not friction in this model. They are its proof of value, displayed to the next buyer.

Which creates a problem no amount of operational excellence can solve. Every capability described in 1.1 and 1.2 — flexible supply, concentrated procurement, elastic output — is a capability for closing that gap.

supply
Limited runs and hidden-edition ratios create a queue. The queue is visible, and the visibility is the marketing.
Step 02
Queue becomes
evidence
Resale premiums and sold-out signage tell the next buyer this object is worth wanting. Desire gets socially confirmed.
Step 03
Demand
compounds
Scarcity draws attention; attention draws demand; demand deepens scarcity. The loop is self-reinforcing and highly profitable.
Step 04
Capacity
answers
The operation scales to meet the queue. It succeeds. The queue disappears — and with it the evidence that made the object worth queueing for.

The paradox in one line: the company's most valuable operational capability is also the mechanism most capable of dismantling its most valuable intangible asset.

Where the trade-off actually sits.

Revenue is units multiplied by realised value. In a scarcity-driven category, realised value is not independent of units — it falls as the object becomes easy to get. That makes the revenue-maximising level of output different from the desirability-maximising level, and it means there is a wrong answer in both directions.

Move the dial. Reported monthly output for the flagship line scaled from roughly ten million units in the first half of 2025 toward a reported fifty million by year end.

The model is deliberately simple: realised value decays exponentially with availability, and revenue is the product of the two. It is not a forecast and contains no company-specific price data. Its purpose is to make the shape of the trade-off visible — in particular, that the revenue curve turns over while output is still rising.

What the reported numbers already show.

The model is a framework. The disclosures are not. Three things happened in FY2025 that are consistent with a category arriving at the far side of that curve.

The concentration figure matters most. A portfolio company that multiplies output on one character, and watches that character's revenue share climb from 23% to 38%, has not diversified its risk. It has converted operating capability into concentration. Every incremental unit of capacity was pointed at the same asset.

So what
The factory did exactly what it was built to do. That is the problem.

There is no operational failure in this chapter. Output scaled, margins expanded, revenue nearly tripled and the supply chain held. Judged as manufacturing, 2025 was close to flawless.

The difficulty is that the asset being manufactured was never the vinyl. It was the difficulty of obtaining the vinyl — and that asset is consumed by precisely the capability the business spent five years perfecting. Scaling a scarce object is less a growth strategy than a liquidation of the thing that made it scarce, executed at very high margins.

Which reframes the valuation question entirely. If the operating engine converts desire into units faster than the IP engine can create new desire, then what an investor underwrites is not a factory. It is the rate at which new characters can be made to matter. That is where 1.4 begins.
